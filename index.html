You cannot test-drive your way to proof of safety.

Automated driving systems do not fail on the average mile. They fail on the rare one — the degraded sensor, the unfamiliar road surface, the condition nobody thought to put in the test plan.

That creates an uncomfortable problem. The events that determine whether a system is safe are precisely the events too rare to observe enough of. OutRes — Outlier Resilience LLC — is an early-stage applied research venture working on that problem.

01

The arithmetic of proving safety by driving

The most-cited result in this field is not a breakthrough. It is a piece of arithmetic. In 2016 Nidhi Kalra and Susan Paddock at RAND asked how many miles an automated vehicle would need to accumulate to demonstrate statistically that it is safer than a human driver. The human benchmark is 1.09 fatalities per 100 million miles.

275M
failure-free miles
To show, at 95% confidence, a fatality rate at the human benchmark.
12.5
years
For a 100-vehicle fleet driving 24/7/365 at 25 mph to cover them.
8.8B
miles
To estimate that rate to within 20% — roughly 400 years for the same fleet.

“This analysis shows that for fatalities it is not possible to test-drive autonomous vehicles to demonstrate their safety to any plausible standard, even if we assume perfect performance.”

Kalra & Paddock, Driving to Safety, RAND Corporation, 2016

This is not an argument against road testing, and it is not a claim that anyone is doing it wrong. It is a statement about what miles can and cannot buy. Accumulating them demonstrates competence in conditions you have already seen. It does not bound the behaviour you have not seen — and past a certain point, adding miles stops changing the answer.

02

The standards name the gap. They do not close it.

Functional safety, as handled by ISO 26262, concerns hazards that arise when something breaks. But an automated driving system can injure someone with every component working exactly as designed — because the design was insufficient for the situation it met.

ISO 21448:2022, the Safety Of The Intended Functionality standard, exists for this case. It defines SOTIF as the absence of unreasonable risk arising from functional insufficiencies of the intended functionality or its implementation — no failure required. It is conventionally framed as four regions: scenarios that are known and safe, known and unsafe, unknown and safe, and the one that matters, unknown and unsafe.

The standard is clear that the engineering objective is to shrink that fourth region. What it cannot supply is a method for demonstrating how much of it you have actually eliminated. You are asked to provide evidence about scenarios you have not enumerated — which is, restated, the same problem as the previous section.

03

The tail is not exotic. It is Tuesday in February.

“Edge case” suggests something bizarre. In practice the conditions that degrade a perception stack are mundane, frequent, and regional — which is exactly why they are easy to under-weight in a test programme assembled somewhere sunny.

Degraded optics

Low winter sun near the horizon, wet glare, spray from the vehicle ahead, a salted and smeared lens. The camera still returns an image. It returns a worse one, and nothing in the pipeline announces that.

Vanishing road structure

Lane markings under snow, slush, or wear. Models that learned to lean on painted lines inherit a dependency that the road does not always honour.

Shifted appearance statistics

A model trained largely on clear conditions meets a distribution it was not fitted to. Degradation here is often graceful-looking and quiet, which makes it worse, not better.

Compounding, not isolated

Real conditions arrive together — dusk and precipitation and a worn surface at once. Test matrices that vary one factor at a time systematically miss the combinations.

04

Finding out late is the expensive way to find out

The structural cost is when the discovery happens. A weakness identified while the data set and model architecture are still open is a design decision. The same weakness surfacing during hardware-in-the-loop or on-road validation is a schedule event — it lands after the tooling, the integration, and the test programme have been built around the assumption that it was not there.

This is the ordinary logic of the V-model, and it is not news to anyone who has run a validation programme. It is worth stating plainly because it sets what a useful contribution to this field would have to do: move evidence earlier, and make it about the conditions you have not sampled rather than the ones you have.
